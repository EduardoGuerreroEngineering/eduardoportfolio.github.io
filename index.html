01BREx · Senior team project

Giving a robot
a sense of place.

BREx Friesian · Autonomous localization & route replay

Combining GPS, IMU, and wheel odometry to estimate the iHerb robot’s position and replay routes across the Cal Poly Pomona campus.

My role

Project lead · Sensor fusion

I led the project, with my main technical work focused on sensor fusion and localization. I also helped across the other subsystems, supporting integration and troubleshooting as the team brought the robot together.

ROS2Extended Kalman filterNav2Jetson Orin
Team outcome

Outdoor localization, waypoint recording, and autonomous route replay—without a base station.

Integrated robot hardwareGPS + IMU + ODOMETRY

A closer look at BREx

Photos and test output from our senior-project posters. Select an image for full size.

Inside the project

My focus: a usable position estimate

GPS noise, IMU drift, and wheel-odometry error made each sensor insufficient on its own. My sensor fusion work brought these inputs into ROS2’s robot_localization Extended Kalman Filter to estimate position, velocity, and orientation.

The Xsens MTi-680G already filters its inertial and GNSS measurements onboard. The robot-level filter combines that sensor data with wheel feedback. The navigation notes also flag overlapping Xsens measurements as a consideration when tuning covariance and investigating GPS jumps.

From global coordinates to a saved route

navsat_transform_node converts GNSS coordinates into a local frame for navigation. A manual datum supported indoor checks when live satellite data was unavailable.

During a manual drive, the recorder saved map-frame position and heading in a YAML file. Nav2 used the saved route and live localization estimate to guide autonomous replay.

Debugging during testing

When the planning area became the limit.

Outdoor replay ran into the bounds of a fixed planning region. The team changed to a rolling costmap and an incremental follower that sent smaller waypoint goals one at a time. Starting near the closest saved waypoint also made route recovery more flexible.

Within my localization work, testing included checking GNSS, odometry, filtered odometry, and coordinate transforms while the robot moved. This connected sensor behavior to what the navigation system was actually receiving.

Understanding the full control loop

Nav2’s velocity commands were converted into virtual joystick input so autonomous and manual driving could share the existing motor-control path. Wheel feedback then returned to the localization system, closing the loop.

As project lead, I supported work across the system. The wider team’s work included the wiring board, motor-control architecture, and perception pipeline.

02Feedback control · Team project

Finding the balance.

Half Tilt · Single-axis ball balancing

An Arduino-controlled lever uses ultrasonic distance feedback and a PID controller to balance a marble.

My role

Assembly, prototyping, programming & debugging

I helped assemble and prototype the mechanism, co-developed the control code, and worked through hardware and software issues during live testing.

Arduino UNOPID controlHC-SR04 sensorMATLAB
Team outcome

A working ball-balancing prototype, with mechanical alignment and close-range sensing limits identified through testing.

Inside the project

Building and programming the feedback loop

The prototype paired an Arduino UNO R3, an HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor, and a 9G servo. Christian Trujillo and I shared the coding work. We moved from a sensor-response test to a reference PID example adapted for our sensor and mechanism, then refined the implementation during testing.

Serial Monitor output exposed the measured distance and commanded servo angle during debugging. I also worked on the MATLAB transfer-function analysis to examine the modeled closed-loop response.

Debugging during testing

Tracing a stalled servo back to the load.

The servo struggled to lift the wooden lever. The team first tried code adjustments, then reduced the lever’s mass, reattached the assembly, and retuned the control code for the changed mechanism.

This was the kind of hands-on debugging I contributed to: testing the prototype, working across assembly and code, and adapting as the physical behavior changed.

What the tests showed

The marble could balance on the lever, but it settled off-center. Uneven motor alignment limited rotation on one side, and the sensor struggled when the marble came too close. The report records these remaining issues along with the successful balancing behavior.

03Digital systems

From logic to gameplay.

FPGA-based Duck Hunt

A game system combining VGA output, sprite rendering, game-state logic, and light-sensor hit detection on a Zybo board.

FPGAVGA graphicsState machines
The outcome

A working game state with a moving white target and an LED that confirmed successful hits.

Inside the project

The challenge

Graphics, game logic, and real-time sensor input had to stay synchronized. Accurate light-sensor detection on the moving target required careful timing and clock management.

The approach

The design separated graphics, memory, input processing, and game logic into modules. Lookup tables stored sprite data, while a finite state machine managed game transitions. A purple LED on the Zybo indicated a registered hit.

04PCB design · Team project

A circuit you can hear.

Custom guitar amplifier

A breadboard prototype developed into a manufactured PCB and custom enclosure, using an LM386 audio amplifier to drive an 8-ohm speaker from a 9V supply.

Collaboration

Built with Gerardo Rodriguez

A shared project covering schematic design, PCB layout, enclosure design, and physical assembly.

Altium DesignerLM386Fusion 360PCB fabrication
Team outcome

A functional guitar amplifier with adjustable gain and volume, housed in a custom 3D-printed enclosure.

Inside the project

From prototype to manufactured board

Our existing circuit worked, but its wiring was difficult to organize. We transferred it into Altium Designer, selected compatible component footprints, checked connections and orientation, and ordered the manufactured PCB from JLCPCB.

Design review caught an LED orientation error before fabrication. Terminal blocks let us place the speaker, audio jack, and potentiometers where they needed to sit in the enclosure.

Making the electronics fit

The enclosure was designed in Fusion 360 around the PCB, speaker, and controls. Board placement and screw-terminal connections made wiring more manageable and helped integrate the electrical and mechanical parts into a working amplifier.
